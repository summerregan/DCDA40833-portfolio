Reflection

When I first saw this dataset on Kaggle about best-selling albums, I was immediately interested. I love all types of music, so it didn’t even feel like I was just picking something random for class. It felt personal. I genuinely wanted to see if any of my favorite albums were on the list, and some actually were. There were also so many classics that my parents used to play for my sister and me in the car growing up. Seeing those albums ranked in a data-driven format made the project feel nostalgic but also analytical at the same time, which I really liked.

Uploading the dataset into Tableau Public was not as seamless as I expected. Even though the file looked clean in Excel, Tableau initially read some of the sales columns as text because of commas in the numbers. I had to go back in and remove formatting, make sure the data types were correct, and clean up a few inconsistencies in artist names. There were also some blank values that needed to be addressed so they wouldn’t create gaps in the visualization. Once I cleaned everything and saved it properly as a .csv, it uploaded correctly. It made me realize that preparing data is just as important as visualizing it.

For my design choices, I focused mainly on total sales as my primary metric because that felt like the clearest way to measure success in this dataset. For dimensions, I used artist, album title, and sometimes year of release. I chose a horizontal bar chart because I wanted comparisons to feel obvious and easy to read. Sorting the bars from highest to lowest made it immediately clear which albums dominated. I also experimented with mapping and different layouts in Tableau, and I honestly really enjoyed playing around with it. It felt creative but structured at the same time.

Going into this, I expected a few iconic albums to completely dominate the chart, and that was mostly confirmed. In the top section of the graph, there's a noticeable gap between the highest-selling albums and the rest. It visually reinforces how certain cultural moments were massive compared to others. Seeing albums my parents played growing up ranked so high also confirmed how influential those records really were beyond just my own nostalgia.

There were definitely some surprises. A few albums I assumed would be near the top were lower than expected. Some artists only appeared once but had extremely high sales compared to artists with multiple entries. I also noticed that more recent albums were not dominating the very top, which probably reflects how streaming has changed music consumption.

For further investigation, I would want to know whether the sales numbers include streaming equivalents or only physical sales. Are the figures adjusted for inflation or population growth? How does genre influence album longevity? And how would this visualization change if we looked at streams instead of traditional album sales?

Overall, I really liked using Tableau Public. It's much more interactive than Excel, and the drag-and-drop system makes experimenting feel easy. I enjoyed mapping things and adjusting views without needing to code anything. That said, embedding and publishing were more complicated than I expected, and there are design limitations compared to tools like Canva. Tableau feels more analytical than aesthetic. Still, it helped me see how data structure shapes storytelling, and I genuinely had fun exploring it.
